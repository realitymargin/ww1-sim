Reasons and sources

Why every odd is what it is, the data behind the war effort, the U-boats and the Americans, and the sources: Broadberry & Harrison (2005), Fayle (1924), Ayres (1919) and the histories of each turning point.

▶ Open the simulator

Model version 3.1 (31 October 2026), sim.mjs. Each odd has three columns: against the Central Powers · middle · for the Central Powers. The video uses the middle column. Every number here is a judgement unless it is marked DATA. Where a judgement rests on a historian's argument, the argument is named. Where I could not find an argument for a number, it says (my judgement).

Every war starts on 28 July 1914, with the war already declared. Whether the July Crisis could have ended without a war is a different question, and this model does not ask it.

Part 1: the data that carries the war between the turning points

The WW2 simulation's viewers said the model was "a spinwheel, not data". So in this model, what happens between the turning points comes from real economic and military figures.

Each power's war effort, each year = its 1913 output × its output in that war year, as a share of 1913 × the share of output its government spent × its army's effectiveness.

Part 3: what the forks change

  • The East-first plan: no march on Paris (no Marne, no Channel ports); France bleeds attacking into Lorraine (× 0.9); Russia takes most of the German army (× 0.85, and three times the chance of a separate peace in 1915).
  • A lost Marne, France still fighting: France's effort × 0.85, Allied will −0.1.
  • The Channel ports: Britain's effort × 0.95; the U-boats sink 15% more.
  • The Ottomans out (never in, or knocked out at Gallipoli): the Straits open, Russia's effort × 1.1, the Tsar less likely to fall (× 0.7), Bulgaria less likely to join.
  • Verdun breaks France: France × 0.7, Allied will −0.2.
  • Jutland: the blockade bites half as fast.
  • The US declares war: Central Powers' will −0.05; its army arrives on the real 1917–18 curve (Ayres 1919).
  • No US by mid-1917: Britain and France run short of dollars, Allied will −0.06 every half-year (Burk, Britain, America and the Sinews of War, 1985).
  • Russia leaves: Ukraine's grain eases the blockade (× 0.8) and Central Powers' will +0.1; about 42 divisions go west.
  • No revolution in Russia: Russia's output stays at its 1916 level (80% of 1913) instead of falling to 68%; the fall in Table 4 came with the revolution.
  • The U-boats break Britain: in the month its fleet can no longer carry the floor of imports. Within a half-year, a collapse of will is taken to come about two months before its end (real history: November 1918), so a break in the last two months waits for the next half-year.
  • A failed Spring Offensive: about 900,000 German casualties by July 1918, Central Powers' will −0.12 and German effort × 0.9.

Part 4: calibration

The will numbers (how much each side loses per half-year) are not historical data. They are tuned so that real history, with every fork played the way it really went, ends in the Central Powers' collapse in the second half of 1918. sim_test.mjs checks that and six other things:

  • Real history ends in the Central Powers' collapse at the end of 1918, on every one of 2,000 seeds.
  • From real history, the 1918 dice give the Central Powers far better odds without the US (7.0%) than with it (1.9%).
  • France knocked out in 1914 → the Central Powers win most wars (82%).
  • Russia stays in and the US comes in → the Central Powers almost never win (under 3%).
  • Convoys on time → Britain is almost never broken by the U-boats (under 5%).
  • No convoys in 1917 → Britain is broken before the war ends in 15–75% of wars (46%).
  • The fleet model reproduces Britain's real ocean-going fleet within 0.6% in every month of 1917 and 1918 (checked when the model is built).
